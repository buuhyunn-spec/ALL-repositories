01The Core Idea: AI Is a Brain, Not a Vending Machine

The biggest mistake beginners make is treating AI like Google — type a few words, take the first answer, done. That's using a brain like a vending machine.

Used correctly, an AI assistant is a thinking partner: something you brainstorm with, delegate drafts to, argue with, and use to check your own work. These tools are all LLMs (large language models) — systems trained on huge amounts of text that predict language. They are prediction machines, not databases, which is why the quality of what you get out depends almost entirely on the quality of what you put in.

  • Vending machine user: "write facebook ad" → gets a generic ad → complains AI is useless.
  • Second-brain user: gives the AI the audience, the offer, the tone, two examples of past winning ads, and asks for 5 variations with different hooks → gets usable drafts in 2 minutes.
Golden rule: An AI can only work with what you give it. Context in = quality out.

02Meet the Five Brains

All five tools are LLM chat assistants. They overlap ~80% in ability — all can write, summarize, brainstorm, code, and analyze. The differences are in their strengths, personality, cost, and data access. A pro keeps 2–3 of them open and routes tasks to the right one.

Made by OpenAI

ChatGPT

  • Best at: all-around generalist work; the "Swiss Army knife."
  • Huge ecosystem: image generation, voice mode, custom GPTs, data analysis.
  • Great default when you're not sure which tool fits.
  • Watch out: can be confidently wrong; verify facts and numbers.
Made by Anthropic

Claude

  • Best at: long documents, careful reasoning, natural writing, and coding.
  • Very large context window — paste entire reports, contracts, or CSV exports.
  • Artifacts: builds working HTML pages, dashboards, and tools inside the chat.
  • Tends to follow nuanced instructions closely; strong for client-facing copy.
Made by xAI

Grok

  • Best at: real-time information — it's plugged into X (Twitter).
  • Use for breaking news, trending topics, and what people are saying right now.
  • More casual, unfiltered tone.
  • Watch out: social media data includes rumors — verify before you act on it.
DeepSeek · Open-source

DeepSeek

  • Best at: logic, math, and coding at very low cost (largely free).
  • Its reasoning models "think out loud" — you can watch the step-by-step logic.
  • Great budget option for heavy analytical work.
  • Watch out: China-based servers — never paste sensitive company or client data.
Made by Moonshot AI

Kimi

  • Best at: very long documents and multi-file analysis with a massive context window.
  • Strong at reading and cross-referencing many uploaded files at once.
  • Free tier is generous — good for research-heavy tasks.
  • Watch out: same data-privacy caution as DeepSeek (China-based servers).

03Prompting: The C.R.A.F.T. Method

Every good prompt has up to five ingredients. You don't need all five every time, but the more of them you include, the better the output. Remember C.R.A.F.T.:

LetterIngredientExample
CContext — background the AI needs"We buy houses for cash in the Bay Area. Our audience is homeowners facing foreclosure."
RRole — who the AI should act as"Act as a direct-response copywriter with 15 years of experience."
AAction — the specific task"Write 5 Facebook ad headlines."
FFormat — how the output should look"Give them as a numbered list, each under 10 words, with a 1-line rationale."
TTone / examples — style guidance or samples"Empathetic, not salesy. Here are 2 past headlines that performed well: …"

Iterate — don't accept draft one

The first output is a starting point. Reply with corrections just like you would with a junior teammate:

  • "Make #3 shorter and remove the exclamation points."
  • "That's too formal. Rewrite like you're texting a friend."
  • "Good structure — now do the same for the plumbing brand."
Pro move — ask the AI to interview you: "Before you write anything, ask me 5 questions that would make this better." This forces out context you forgot to give.

04Hallucinations: When the Brain Makes Things Up

All LLMs sometimes hallucinate — they state false information fluently and confidently. They can invent statistics, fake URLs, nonexistent laws, and imaginary case studies. Confidence of tone means nothing: hallucinations sound just as sure as facts.

Two related concepts you must know:

  • Context window: how much text the model can consider at once. Bigger window = can handle longer documents and conversations.
  • Training cutoff: models are trained on data up to a certain date. They don't know what happened after it unless they have live web search.

Highest-risk outputs — always verify before using:

  • Specific numbers, statistics, and prices
  • Legal, tax, and compliance claims
  • Quotes, citations, and links
  • Anything that happened recently

Verification tactics:

  1. Cross-examine: ask "How confident are you? What could be wrong here?"
  2. Second opinion: paste the answer into a different AI and ask it to fact-check.
  3. Source check: use a tool with web search, or Google the claim yourself. Click every link the AI cites — fake citations are common.
  4. Human check: anything going to a client or to Juan gets human eyes first. Always.
Never publish AI-generated facts, stats, or legal claims without verifying them. "The AI said so" is not a defense.

05Data Privacy: What Never Goes Into a Chatbot

Assume anything you paste into a public AI tool could be stored on the provider's servers and possibly used for training. Treat prompts like postcards, not sealed letters.

Never paste into any AI tool:

  • Client names combined with financial or property distress details (PII)
  • Passwords, API keys, or login credentials — even when debugging
  • Unreleased company financials or contracts with identifying details

Safe practice — anonymize first: "A homeowner in San Mateo behind on payments" instead of a real name and address. Replace real numbers with rounded placeholders when the exact figure isn't needed for the task.

Be extra strict with tools hosted outside the US (DeepSeek, Kimi) — use them for generic reasoning and public information, not company data.

06Workflows: Making the Brains Work Together

The Draft → Critique → Polish chain

  1. Draft in Tool A ("Write a first draft of…").
  2. Critique in Tool B ("Act as a harsh editor. Find weaknesses in this draft: …").
  3. Polish back in Tool A (or yourself) using the critique.

Different models have different blind spots — using one to review another catches errors a single model misses.

The Summarize → Decide pattern

Long report or messy data? Paste it into a long-context tool (Claude/Kimi) and ask: "Summarize in 10 bullets, then list the 3 decisions this data suggests, with the strongest evidence for each."

The Brainstorm → Filter pattern

Ask for volume first ("Give me 20 ideas, don't filter"), then ask the AI to rank its own list against your criteria ("Now score each 1–10 on cost and speed to implement, and defend the top 3").

07When NOT to Use AI

  • Final authority decisions — pricing, hiring, legal commitments. AI informs; humans decide.
  • As your only fact source for anything high-stakes.
  • Sensitive communications — apologies, conflict, bad news. People can tell.
  • When you haven't read the output. Sending unread AI output under your name is the fastest way to lose trust.
Accountability rule: If your name is on it, you own every word — no matter which brain drafted it.
